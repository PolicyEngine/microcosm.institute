microcosm / UK methodology

Household data · United Kingdom

How Microcosm builds UK household data

Microcosm combines household survey records with income, wealth and spending data, then calibrates their weights against official statistics. Its local pipeline gives each household record one location and one weight, shared across national, constituency and local-authority estimates.

01 · Overview

From survey records to a weighted population

A microsimulation dataset represents households through records of their members, relationships, incomes and other characteristics. Each household has a weight: the number of households it represents in the population. PolicyEngine applies tax and benefit rules to these records and uses their weights to estimate population outcomes.

Microcosm starts with the Family Resources Survey (FRS) and adds income, wealth and spending information from other sources. Throughout, it keeps the links between people, their benefit units and their households. A benefit unit is a single adult or a couple and any dependent children: the family group that means-tested benefits such as Universal Credit assess together.

The completed records then follow one of two routes. The national route calibrates their weights to UK, country and regional statistics. The local route first copies each household and places every copy in a small census area, then calibrates one set of weights against national and local statistics together. It can also select a smaller, fixed number of records and refit their weights before packaging; these reduced exports are checked again before release review. [2]

02 · Sources

What each source contributes

The FRS supplies the household structure and reported characteristics. Other sources add information that the FRS does not collect in enough detail or represent adequately on its own. The source manifest pins the files and versions a build must use; the vintages below are those of the implementation this page describes. [3]

Additional stages use the National Travel Survey for bus use; the Effects of Taxes and Benefits dataset for VAT and for the value of education, rail and bus subsidies; National Health Service (NHS) activity and cost tables for health-service use; published take-up statistics for whether eligible families claim benefits; house-price statistics to update property values; and published statistics and research for capital gains, pension salary sacrifice and student loans. The manifest records each stage’s coverage and assumptions. For example, recent National Travel Survey microdata cover England only, so bus use in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland is predicted as if households lived in a stand-in English region. Bus fares in Scotland and Northern Ireland are then priced from their own published statistics, while Welsh fares keep their survey-based values. [3]

Imputation adds characteristics; calibration changes representation

Microcosm uses statistical imputation, including weighted quantile regression forests, to draw values conditional on shared characteristics such as age, sex, income, household composition, tenure and region; the predictors differ by stage. Each value is drawn at random from the predicted distribution rather than set to its average, so otherwise similar households still differ. The draws do not link an FRS household to the same identifiable household in another survey.

The income stage keeps the incomes reported on the original FRS records. It also adds copies of a random sample of FRS households, gives the adults in those copies incomes drawn from the Survey of Personal Incomes, and redraws their housing costs and benefits to match. Because the FRS contains few households with very high incomes, further copies give one adult an income drawn within one of HMRC’s income bands from £200,000 upwards, with starting weights set from HMRC’s published taxpayer counts. These records give calibration enough high-income households to work with. [3]

Added records take their starting weights from the households they copy or from their region, so the total starting weight is unchanged. Wealth and spending stages then draw related variables in sequence. Calibration then changes how many households each completed record represents.

03 · Geography

One location for each household record

The FRS identifies a household’s region but does not provide a representative sample for every constituency. In the local build, Microcosm makes 15 identical copies of each completed household, keeping its people and family relationships together, and splits the household’s weight equally among them. Each copy receives one small census area within its original FRS region, drawn with probability proportional to the area’s census household count. The draws are reproducible: each is tied to the record’s identity, so rebuilding with the same inputs gives the same assignment. [2]

The assignment uses 2021 Census Output Areas in England and Wales, 2022 Census Output Areas in Scotland, and 2021 Census Data Zones in Northern Ireland. Published, versioned lookups map each area to one constituency, one local authority and other geographic labels; in Great Britain, an area that straddles a boundary is assigned wholly to one side by the official best-fit lookup. The constituency geography uses the 2024 boundaries; the local-authority geography uses the April 2023 definition. [4]

Each resulting household record has one location and one weight. The same record and weight contribute to its constituency, its local authority and the national total. Constituencies and local authorities overlap, so Microcosm derives both labels from the small area rather than assuming that one sits inside the other.

These assignments create a modelled local population. They do not recover respondents’ actual addresses or establish that a record describes a particular small area accurately. The local calibration described below fits constituency and local-authority statistics; small-area assignment alone does not validate ward or neighbourhood estimates.

04 · Calibration

One set of weights, fitted across geographic levels

For each target statistic, Microcosm calculates every household’s contribution and compares the weighted total with the official value. A target might count people in an age band, sum employment income among taxpayers, or count families receiving Universal Credit. The local build fits national, regional, constituency and local-authority targets in one optimisation. [5]

National and regional targets include population, household, income, tax and benefit statistics, along with housing, student-loan, spending and other administrative totals. Targets have defined populations and reference periods; HMRC income statistics, for example, describe taxpayers rather than all residents. Targets refer to 2025, and the build records which targets it fitted, which it excluded, and how it brought source values to that year. Available targets and coverage differ across the UK’s countries. [5]

Reconciling overlapping statistics

Published totals for the same concept can differ across geographic levels because of reference periods, definitions or census disclosure controls. Before fitting weights, Microcosm reconciles targets that measure the same thing at more than one level. Where a higher-level total is fitted for the UK, Great Britain, a country or a region, it rescales the constituency and local-authority targets beneath that total to match it. Where there is no such total, it rescales local-authority targets to the constituency totals in the same region or nation. It records every adjustment. The rule applies only to matching measurements and a short list of declared equivalences; it does not combine statistics with incompatible populations. [6]

Fitting with constraints

A gradient-based optimiser (Adam) adjusts the weights to reduce the relative error between weighted totals and their targets. Each target’s contribution is capped, so a single badly missed target cannot dominate the fit. National targets (including the regional rows), constituency targets and local-authority targets each receive an equal share of the objective, so the much larger number of local targets does not overwhelm the national ones. No calibrated weight can exceed ten times its weight at the start of calibration; this is a hard limit applied throughout the fit, with no matching lower limit. The total number of households is fitted as a target rather than held fixed. These constraints can leave residual discrepancies, which the diagnostics report. [7]

For a policy comparison, PolicyEngine calculates baseline and reform outcomes for the same household records. An area’s total income change sums each assigned household’s change multiplied by its weight. With the same dataset and population definition, constituency totals sum to the national weighted total. An average divides by the relevant weighted household count; averages and percentages do not add across areas.

05 · Enhanced FRS

What changes from Enhanced FRS

Enhanced FRS, built in the policyengine-uk-data repository, is the UK dataset that PolicyEngine currently certifies for policy analysis. Its current release (1.58.0) also starts from the FRS 2024–25 and draws on the same families of surveys for income, wealth and spending. For local estimates, it fits two separate weight matrices, one for constituencies and one for local authorities. In each, a household carries a separately fitted weight for every area in its country, while its characteristics, and the taxes and benefits calculated from them, stay the same in every area. Recent releases also give each household one output area within its FRS region, but the local weight matrices are not tied to that location. [8]

For example, a household outside London can contribute to a London constituency estimate while keeping the outside-London benefit cap, because PolicyEngine-UK reads the household’s region when calculating the cap. [9] In Microcosm, each copy of a household stays in its original FRS region and carries one weight, so region- and country-based rules match the areas the copy counts towards. Microcosm also sets PolicyEngine-UK’s local-authority input from the assigned area. Some finer inputs still come from the source household or are drawn within its region, including the Broad Rental Market Area used for Local Housing Allowance and the household’s council-tax bill.

Microcosm fits national, regional, constituency and local-authority targets with one weight per record in a single optimisation, whereas Enhanced FRS fits its constituency and local-authority matrices separately, each alongside national targets. Microcosm also places 15 copies of each household so that each area has more records to weight, adds census household-count targets, reconciles overlapping targets across levels under a declared order, limits how far any weight can rise, and records local support and release checks. These changes improve geographic consistency and make the assumptions easier to inspect. They do not, on their own, establish that every local estimate is more accurate; the release process requires a measured comparison with Enhanced FRS. [1]

06 · Evidence

Read calibration fit alongside local support

Calibration diagnostics report discrepancies for individual targets and groups of targets. A headline percentage of targets within a tolerance describes fit to those targets. It does not measure the accuracy of every household characteristic or policy result.

The local pipeline also runs rotated holdouts. It refits the weights five times, each time leaving out one of five groups of local targets and measuring how well the refit matches them, while national targets stay in every fit; each local target is held out once. This checks performance on local statistics that the refit did not directly target. It is not fully independent external validation, because the omitted targets can share sources and information with the fitted targets. [10]

Support diagnostics count records with positive weights, distinct source households, and effective sample size within each area. Effective sample size summarises weight concentration: it falls when a few records carry much of an area’s weight. Copied households do not create additional independent survey observations. Record counts and effective sample size should therefore be read alongside the number of underlying source households. [10]

Release checks assess source provenance, household relationships, geography, calibration quality, local support and the exported dataset. The national and local release processes also require comparison evidence against Enhanced FRS. Passing software tests or completing an optimisation does not establish that a dataset has passed those release checks. Any published fit figures must identify the dataset release, target scope and diagnostic evidence they describe. [1]

Limits on interpretation

  • Finite survey support. Rare household types and narrowly defined groups can have little underlying evidence, even after copying and calibration.
  • Imputed relationships. Characteristics drawn from different sources depend on the predictors, coverage and assumptions used to connect them.
  • Untargeted outcomes. Fitting population, income or Universal Credit counts does not guarantee local accuracy for disability, savings, every benefit or every reform.
  • Inputs that do not follow the assigned area. Some model inputs that vary within a region, such as the Broad Rental Market Area used for Local Housing Allowance and council-tax bills, come from the source household rather than the assigned small area.
  • Coverage differs by nation. Northern Ireland has no local Universal Credit or council-tax targets, and its household wealth is imputed using the pattern for Wales.
  • Model and policy assumptions. Local impacts also depend on tax and benefit rules, benefit take-up and the policy comparison. The dataset pipeline does not itself establish behavioural responses or confidence intervals.

07 · Version and references

A methodology that can be cited and checked

This draft covers the FRS 2024–25 implementation at Microcosm revision e3e3d881f0fdaf8d4796dea25ed4b72deb52cb6e. It does not report measured performance for a named dataset release. A reproducible policy analysis should also identify its dataset release, PolicyEngine-UK version, policy year and reform.

The pipeline code and methodology references are public. Several underlying surveys require licensed access; publishing the methodology does not make their respondent-level data freely redistributable. [3]
