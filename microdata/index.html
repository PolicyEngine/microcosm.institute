Where microdata lives.

microcosm builds simulation-ready synthetic populations from two ingredients: raw household microdata and published administrative facts. This page maps the first ingredient — which countries make household income microdata available, to whom, and on what terms.

02 — open download ● anyone, at most a free account

The tier the US made famous — and the nineteen other rows in it.

Data a person, company, or lab can obtain directly. The United States is the famous case, but it has company: most of the Americas, two EU member states, and much of Asia publish simulation-grade income microdata this way.

03 — licensed ◐ application first, then off-site use

Free to use, forbidden to share: Europe's standard regime.

Real microdata that leaves the building — after an application, a licence, or organisational accreditation. Free in most cases; the gate is institutional identity, not money. Every licence in this tier prohibits redistributing the data, and most restrict what can be published from derivatives.

04 — secure enclave ○ the data never leaves

The best data in the world, and it never leaves the building.

Full-population income, tax, and benefit registers under the tightest architecture: remote analysis environments where unit records are never seen or downloaded. In several of these countries the national tax–benefit model is itself a service inside the enclave.

05 — the synthetic turn

Real records stay inside; the public product is a model of them.

The most consequential change on this map is not where the walls are — it is what statistical agencies are doing about them. Five have adopted synthetic release as an official mechanism:

IRS (United States). The traditional Public Use File of individual tax returns — sold under licence for decades — has been withdrawn; even the historical files are no longer distributed. The announced replacement is a fully synthetic PUF.

Eurostat. Publishes fully synthetic EU-SILC public use files for all 27 member states under CC BY 4.0 — while disclaiming them for statistical inference.

Statistics Canada. Has shipped SPSD/M since the late 1980s: a synthetic household database statistically fused from tax returns, employment-insurance records, and surveys, distributed free under licence.

Australian Taxation Office. A synthetic version of its ALife tax panel is in development, intended for use outside the secure environment.

ONS (United Kingdom). Maintains a synthetic data policy holding that synthetic versions "can be shared with less restriction than real data," with release decided by each dataset's owner.

The pattern: real records stay inside; the public product is a statistical model of them. What no agency yet publishes is a synthetic file that is certified accurate — Eurostat's own public files carry the opposite warranty. That gap is the problem microcosm exists to work on: calibrated synthetic populations, built from whatever tier a country offers, validated against held-out administrative facts, and published as openly as each source licence allows.
